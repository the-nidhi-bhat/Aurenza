Decision support for perishable produce

Every crate is on a clock. Decide where it goes before the clock runs out.

Aurenza turns a day of produce — batches with hours of shelf life left, destinations with demand, price and capacity — into a dispatch ledger you can defend line by line. It then re-runs itself against a first-expired-first-out baseline over 120 disruption scenarios and shows exactly where it wins, ties, and loses.

The problem

A dashboard that only reports is not decision support.

Perishable stock loses value by the hour, and the loss is uneven: a crate with two days of shelf life is not the same asset as one with two hours. First-expired-first-out gets the ordering right and the economics wrong — it moves the oldest pallet to the nearest buyer even when the margin, the distance or the demand says otherwise.

What an operator actually needs is a plan they can stand behind: which batch, to which destination, at what discount, for how much expected contribution — and a sentence saying why. Anything less is a spreadsheet with better typography.

How it works

Four steps, every one of them inspectable.

  1. Model the day.

    Batches carry harvested mass, remaining shelf life and grade; destinations carry demand, price, capacity and distance. Both sides are plain data, so any assumption can be swapped and re-run.

  2. Search the best feasible destination.

    Perishability sets the priority, contribution sets the tie-break, and hard constraints — capacity, demand, freshness floor, distance — rule candidates out before they ever reach the plan.

  3. Price and explain.

    Each dispatch carries a discount, an effective price per kilogram, an expected contribution and a plain-language reason, so the ledger reads like an argument rather than a number dump.

  4. Prove it.

    The same input is solved by the FEFO baseline and by the planner, then scored across disruption scenarios and reconciled — quantity, revenue and spoilage accounted for, not just plotted.

What is in the box

Built to be audited, not admired.

Perishability-first batching

Shelf life, not arrival order, decides which batch moves first — and how hard it has to be priced to move.

Best-feasible destination search

Candidates are filtered by capacity, demand and freshness floor before contribution is compared.

Markdown that keeps the margin

A discount is applied to recover contribution from stock that would otherwise be written off, and the effective price per kilogram is shown every time.

Feasibility guardrails

Allocations that violate a constraint are never emitted. Across the evaluated scenarios, both plans stay feasible on every run.

Every dispatch explained

Each line states the batch, the destination, the discount and the decision reason in words a dispatcher can repeat to a customer.

Reproducible evaluation

Scenarios run from fixed seeds, and the committed results file is re-derived from the engine by the test suite — so the numbers cannot silently drift.

Evidence, including the uncomfortable parts

What the evaluation actually shows.

Over 30 seeds and 120 disruption scenarios, the planner beats the FEFO baseline on net revenue in 115 runs, with a median improvement of 24.9% and no infeasible allocation on either side. That is the win.

The honest half: spoilage is unchanged from the baseline in 118 of the 120 scenarios — the planner holds it rather than beating it, and cutting spoilage further is the next milestone. Demand fulfilment also lands slightly below baseline on average, because the planner spends some service level to convert at-risk stock into revenue. Both facts are printed in the console rather than buried, and every scenario is inspectable.

Source: demo/evaluation.json (mode standard), engine-generated synthetic scenarios — not real market data.
